Customer shopping & promotions.

01 / THE BUSINESS QUESTIONS

What sells, and what do promotions change?

Our group examined which products customers choose and how promotion exposure relates to purchase value. We used SQL for data cleaning and ETL, joined customer and purchase records, and analyzed the results in Excel to propose inventory priorities and promotion tests.

Group project by David Zvi Samuel Alter, Shilat Ben Shimol Biton, and Hagit Turjeman, completed as part of the Data & AI Analyst Program.

02 / THE WORKFLOW

SQL preparation. Excel analysis. Business recommendations.

01 / SQL

Clean and connect the data

Used SQL to clean and prepare the source data as part of an ETL workflow, then joined customer attributes with purchase details by Customer ID to create the dataset for Excel.

02 / EXCEL

Define and compare the KPIs

Use PivotTables and formulas to compare product popularity, revenue share, ratings, and promotion exposure. Segment results by product category, customer group, and season.

03 / BUSINESS INSIGHTS

Turn findings into actions

Recommend where to focus stock and product reviews, identify data patterns that affect interpretation, and propose controlled promotion tests.

03 / PRODUCT PREFERENCES

Identify the winners and the exception.

Jewelry, Pants, and Blouse lead the popularity index. Jeans ranks last, around 84 points below the next-lowest product, making it a focused candidate for a pricing, placement, or product review.

04 / CATEGORY REVENUE

A large category is not the same as a stronger product.

76.6%of recorded revenue comes from Clothing and Accessories.

These categories contain 19 of the 25 product types. Revenue per product is much closer across categories, ranging from about $9,023 to $9,479. Category size helps explain why total revenue differs so much.

05 / PROMOTIONS

More promotion exposure does not establish higher spending.

43% of purchases include a promotion. Their average value is $59.28, compared with $60.13 without a promotion, an observed difference of −1.4%.

The pattern varies by category. Footwear shows a positive difference, making it a candidate for a controlled test rather than a reason to assume every promotion works.

Two fields. The same group.

Every promo-code purchase also has a discount. The fields match in all 3,900 records, so we interpret them together as promotion exposure.

06 / RECOMMENDATIONS

What we would test next.

PRODUCTS

Support demand, investigate Jeans

Keep the leading products visible and stocked. Review Jeans pricing, placement and feedback before deciding whether to change or replace the product.

PROMOTIONS

Start a controlled Footwear test

Compare a targeted offer with a control group. Measure conversion, purchase value and margin to evaluate incremental value rather than relying on group averages.

DATA QUALITY

Check how offers are assigned

Investigate why the promotion fields are identical and why exposure differs by customer group. Validate these rules before expanding a campaign.

08 / METHODOLOGY & LIMITS

Keep the conclusion tied to the evidence.

This synthetic dataset is useful for practicing an analysis workflow. It describes recorded purchases and group differences, rather than real business impact or the causal effect of an offer.

Metric definitions and source checks
  • Popularity index: purchase count × average review rating for each product type.
  • Revenue share: category purchase amount / $233,081 total purchase amount. No cost or margin information is supplied.
  • Promotion exposure: purchases with both promo-code and discount fields set to Yes / all purchases. The two fields are identical here.
  • Purchase-value difference: (average amount with exposure / average amount without exposure − 1) × 100. This is an association between observed groups.
  • Ratings comparison: Pearson correlation across 25 product types, using average rating and purchase count.
  • Source reconciliation: figures on this page were recomputed from the workbook’s source rows and reconciled with the two SQL tables. The original workbook and presentation are provided unchanged.
How strongly do ratings relate to purchase count?

Across 25 product types, the Pearson correlation between average rating and purchase count is r = −0.15. This weak relationship suggests that ratings alone are a poor basis for inventory decisions in this dataset.

Where the supplied summaries differ from the source rows

The source rows give Outerwear the highest purchase count per product (162), while slide 7 names Footwear. The lowest revenue per product on slide 8 belongs to Footwear ($9,023), not Accessories. For subscription status, the source rows show exposure for all 1,053 subscribers and 624 of 2,847 non-subscribers; slide 16 reports different figures. The workbook’s Discount Impact summary also contains formula errors. The website uses recalculated source values for these comparisons.
