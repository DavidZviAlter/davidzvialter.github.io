Customer shopping and promotions

Joined two SQL tables into one clean dataset, then measured which categories drive revenue and whether promotions raise spend.

What sells, and what do promotions change?

Our group looked at which products customers choose, and how promotion exposure relates to what they spend. We cleaned and joined the data in SQL, analysed it in Excel, and turned the results into stock priorities and promotion tests.

Group project by David Zvi Samuel Alter, Shilat Ben Shimol Biton and Hagit Turjeman, part of the Data & AI Analyst Program.

MySQL

Clean and connect the data

Cleaned and prepared the source data, then joined customer attributes to purchase details by Customer ID to build one dataset for Excel.

Excel

Define and compare the KPIs

PivotTables and formulas compare product popularity, revenue share, ratings and promotion exposure, by category, customer group and season.

Insights

Turn findings into actions

Where to focus stock and product reviews, which data patterns change the reading, and which promotion tests to run.

The winners, and one exception.

Jewelry, Pants and Blouse lead the popularity index. Jeans ranks last, about 84 points below the next-lowest product, which makes it a focused candidate for a pricing, placement or product review.

A large category is not a stronger product.

76.6% of recorded revenue comes from Clothing and Accessories, but those two categories hold 19 of the 25 product types. Revenue per product is close everywhere, from about $9,023 to $9,479. Category size explains most of the gap.

More promotions did not mean higher spend.

43% of purchases include a promotion. Their average value is $59.28, against $60.13 without one. Footwear is the exception, which makes it the place to run a controlled test.

Every promo-code purchase also carries a discount: the two fields match in all 3,900 records, so we read them together as one exposure.

What we would test next.

Products

Support demand, investigate Jeans

Keep the leading products visible and stocked. Review Jeans pricing, placement and feedback before changing or replacing it.

Promotions

Run a controlled Footwear test

Compare a targeted offer with a control group, and measure conversion, purchase value and margin in each.

Data quality

Check how offers are assigned

Find out why the promotion fields are identical and why exposure differs by customer group, before expanding any campaign.

Keep the conclusion tied to the evidence.

A synthetic dataset is good practice for the workflow. It describes recorded purchases and group differences; measuring the real effect of an offer takes a live test.

Metric definitions and source checks
  • Popularity index: purchase count × average review rating for each product type.
  • Revenue share: category purchase amount / $233,081 total. No cost or margin data is supplied.
  • Promotion exposure: purchases with both promo-code and discount set to Yes, over all purchases.
  • Purchase-value difference: (average with exposure / average without − 1) × 100. An association between observed groups.
  • Source reconciliation: every figure here was recomputed from the workbook's source rows and checked against the two SQL tables.
Do ratings predict sales?
  • Across 25 product types, the Pearson correlation between average rating and purchase count is r = −0.15. Ratings alone are a weak basis for stock decisions in this dataset.
Where the slides differ from the source rows
  • The source rows give Outerwear the highest purchases per product (162); slide 7 names Footwear.
  • The lowest revenue per product on slide 8 belongs to Footwear ($9,023), not Accessories.
  • Subscription status: the rows show exposure for all 1,053 subscribers and 624 of 2,847 non-subscribers; slide 16 reports different figures.
