02 / THE BUSINESS QUESTION

How should an investor
balance return and risk?

I analyzed movie performance to develop two investment strategies for different risk preferences. By comparing average ROI with the spread of returns, I looked beyond the highest average to understand the uncertainty behind it.

The results pointed to a clear trade-off: comedy offered lower volatility, while drama combined a higher average return with much greater variation in outcomes.

03 / MY APPROACH

From source data to a recommendation.

01

Define KPIs and prepare the data

Defined the KPIs: average ROI, ROI standard deviation, and ROI over risk. Used an Excel ETL workflow to combine source data using lookup functions, clean and standardize fields, and create budget and runtime groups for analysis.

02

Compare the trade-offs

Used PivotTables to compare genres, actors, directors, budgets, runtimes, content ratings, and release months. Examined average ROI alongside its standard deviation.

03

Communicate the decision

Built a presentation around two investment strategies, explaining why higher returns do not automatically make an option more attractive.

05 / A DECISION IN DETAIL

Why I preferred Steve Carell.

The highest average wasn’t my only criterion.

Joseph Gordon-Levitt had the higher average ROI: 461.59%, compared with Steve Carell’s 432.94%. Carell’s lower variability produced the stronger ROI-over-risk ratio, which better suited the conservative strategy.

07 / METHODOLOGY & LIMITS

What the numbers can tell us.

The useful result is a framework for comparing trade-offs. Group averages are a starting point for investigation, rather than a prediction of a new movie’s performance.

Metric definitions and data scope
  • ROI proxy: (Gross revenue − Budget) / Budget × 100. This excludes unrecorded costs and revenue sharing, so it is not the investor’s net return.
  • Risk: the sample standard deviation of ROI, expressed in percentage points. It measures variability, not the probability of a loss.
  • ROI over risk: mean ROI / standard deviation. This is a descriptive comparison ratio, not a Sharpe ratio.
  • Scope: the workbook contains 3,725 source rows. The displayed genre results reproduce its 2005–2015 analysis across available countries and languages. The presentation proposes a U.S./English focus, but those filters do not reproduce the saved genre averages.
  • Sample sizes: the six compared genres have more than 100 records each in the workbook’s genre count. The highlighted actor and director comparisons use at least three records per person.
  • Budget boundaries: the workbook formula uses low < $10M, medium $10M to < $40M, high $40M to $100M inclusive, and blockbuster > $100M.
Limitations and what I would investigate next
  • Very large returns from a few low-budget films can dominate averages. Medians, distributions, and outlier sensitivity would strengthen the comparison.
  • The analysis does not establish that a specific actor, director, release month, or runtime causes higher returns.
  • The source’s actor field and small samples limit what can be inferred about “star power.”
  • Audience and critic scores become available after release and should not be treated as information known at an investment decision.
  • A next iteration would standardize filters across views, validate the revenue definition and source duplicates, and evaluate recommendations on a later time period.
